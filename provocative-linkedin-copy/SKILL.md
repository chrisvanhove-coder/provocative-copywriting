# Provocative LinkedIn Copy

This skill turns ideas into LinkedIn posts that stop the scroll, hold the reader to the last line, and leave a clear point of view behind. It fuses three crafts:

- **Copywriting hooks**: earn the "see more" click.
- **Shakespeare's dramatic structure**: turn a post into a small play with a turn in the middle.
- **Journalism**: find the real story, prove it, and cut everything else.

The governing idea: **provocation is a tool for earning attention for an idea that deserves it.** The claim is provocative. The tone is never cheap. Every hot line must be paid off with a real insight, a real number, or a real scene.

## 1. Intake: ask only what's missing

Before writing, check whether the author has given you these. Ask only for what's missing, in one short message (use a multiple-choice question tool if available), never more than 3 questions:

1. **Raw material**: the idea, story, observation, draft or voice note.
2. **Edge level (1–5)**: see the dial in section 2. If they don't answer, use **3** and say so.
3. **Primary goal for this post**: leads, reach, or authority. Pick one; it decides the structure (section 5) and the CTA (section 8).
4. **Receipts**: any real number, scene, quote or result they can stand behind. Never invent one (section 9).

If they have already given enough, don't ask. State your assumptions in one line at the top of the deliverable and write.

## 2. The edge dial

The author sets the heat per post. Each level changes the *claim*, not the politeness.

| Level | Name | What it does | Shape example (same idea) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Spark | A surprising observation, gently reframed | "Most visitors skim your product page. They remember one sentence." |
| 2 | Contrarian | Challenges a belief the industry holds | "Product pages are written for the team, not the buyer." |
| 3 | Sharp | Names a bad practice and takes a side; some readers will disagree | "Your feature list is the least persuasive part of your website." |
| 4 | Polarizing | Calls out a convention head-on; the reader must pick a side | "Most B2B websites are written to impress competitors, not to help buyers." |
| 5 | Heat | Taboo, confession or shock opener; deliberately uncomfortable | "Nobody has ever bought anything because of a mission statement." (only with data behind it) |

**Why the dial matters (research):** controversy increases discussion at low levels, but past a *moderate* level, more controversy *reduces* discussion, because discomfort starts to outweigh interest (Chen & Berger, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 2013). Anonymity and talking with friends lower that discomfort. LinkedIn is the opposite: real names, colleagues and strangers watching. So on LinkedIn the sweet spot for comments is usually **2–4**.

Rules of thumb:
- **Lead-generation posts: 2–3.** Buyers must feel safe agreeing with you in public.
- **Reach posts: 3–4.**
- **Level 5: occasionally, and only with receipts.** The hotter the claim, the stronger the proof. A level-5 line without evidence reads as a stunt and costs authority.
- Emotion matters as much as heat. High-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) travel further than low-arousal ones like sadness, and positive content spreads more than negative overall (Berger & Milkman, 2012). Aim for **awe** (a surprising truth) or **productive frustration** (a convention everyone secretly hates). Avoid gloom.

**Provoke ideas, not people.** Target conventions, practices, beliefs, briefs, rituals. Never mock a named person or company, or the customers who are the author's audience. Section 5 shows how Mark Antony demolished his opponents without ever insulting them.

After the post, offer the hook and the turn one level **hotter** and one level **cooler**, so the author can adjust the dial.

## 3. Find the story (the journalist's first job)

Most weak posts fail before the first word: they have a topic but no story.

**The 25-word test.** Write, for yourself, what this post is about and why a stranger should care, in 25 words or fewer. If you can't, the idea isn't ready. Ask the author one sharpening question instead of writing mush.

**Name the enemy.** Every provocative post argues *against* something: a belief, a habit, a lazy standard. Write it down: "The enemy is ___." Without an enemy there is no conflict, and without conflict there is no story.

**Run the news-values check.** Journalists pick stories by these. The more a post hits, the stronger it is:
- **Conflict**: two forces or beliefs collide.
- **The unusual**: weird, counterintuitive, "man bites dog".
- **Impact**: it changes money, time or results for the reader.
- **Timeliness**: why now? A season, a trend, something that happened this week.
- **Human interest**: a person, a moment, a feeling.
- **Proximity**: it touches the reader's own job ("if you write job ads...").
- **Prominence**: a known brand or category (discuss the category, don't attack the brand).

**Angle mines** (build these from section 0):
- The author's core thesis, and the belief it argues against.
- Field notes: something they saw, heard or tried this week.
- What their data or client work is teaching them, including what they got wrong.
- Their outsider view: a background, country or industry that lets them notice what insiders no longer see.
- Research that supports or challenges their thesis (keep a bank of it; see section 12).

## 4. The hook: the first ~140 characters

LinkedIn cuts posts off at roughly **140 characters on mobile** and about **210 on desktop** before "see more". The hook and the line after it must land inside that window. Nothing else matters if they don't.

**Sugarman's law of the slippery slide:** the only job of the first sentence is to get the second sentence read. The second's job is to get the third read. Keep the first line short, easy, and almost incomplete, so the reader has to continue.

**What the data says** (MagicPost analysis of ~1.2M LinkedIn posts, June 2026; correlations, not controlled experiments):
- **Statements beat questions.** First lines containing a question earned 19 median likes vs 29 for the rest (−34%), and fewer comments too. The gap held at every follower size. Sugarman gave the same advice decades earlier: don't ask questions, make true statements.
- **Numbers help.** First lines with a number earned 35 median likes vs 26 without. Specificity reads as proof.
- **Short wins.** First lines of 1–10 words did best; 11–15 words did worst.

So the default hook is **a short, specific statement with tension in it.** A question must earn its place: sharp, specific, and impossible to answer "no" to and scroll on.

### Hook families

Write at least **5 hooks from different families**, then pick the strongest. The examples show the *shape* only; always rebuild them from the author's real material.

1. **The verdict** (contrarian claim): "Your onboarding email is a terms-of-service page with a logo."
2. **The number**: "[N] of [N] customers we interviewed couldn't say what we do." (Real numbers only.)
3. **The cold open** (Shakespeare's tragedies open mid-disturbance): drop the reader into a scene. "The client stopped reading at slide three. I watched her do it."
4. **The confession** (a soliloquy, the private voice made public): "I run a content agency. I haven't posted in four months."
5. **The spoiler** (the *Romeo and Juliet* prologue gives away the ending in the first 14 lines, and audiences still watch to see *how*): "This launch will fail. I knew it from the brief."
6. **The antithesis** (Shakespeare's favourite figure: "Fair is foul, and foul is fair"): "Brands explain what they make. Customers buy what it makes them feel."
7. **The enemy**: "Best-practice templates are why every website sounds the same."
8. **The reframe** (the Sonnet 130 move: refuse the flattering convention): "Your customer doesn't want a project manager. She wants her Sunday back."
9. **The proximity call-out**: "If you write job ads, your best candidates stopped reading at line two."
10. **The prediction**: "In five years, nobody will write 'passionate' on a CV."

**Two-line hooks work best:** line 1 grabs, line 2 twists or raises the stakes. Combine at most two families (e.g. number + verdict). Three or more gets muddy.

**Hook test**, reject any hook that fails one:
- Could the reader predict the rest of the post from it? Then it's weak.
- Is it specific: a number, a name of a thing, a sensory detail?
- Does it contain tension (an enemy, a contradiction, a gap)?
- Is it true, and does the post pay it off? A curiosity gap the post doesn't close is a betrayal, and readers remember.
- Would the author say it out loud to the most senior person in their audience? If not, it's too cheap, not too bold.
- Does the hook itself match the dial? A hook that breaks a belief nobody holds is level 1–2, however sharp the body is. Hedges like "barely" or "might" cool it further. When the author asks for provocative, make sure the first line names or challenges something readers actually do or believe.

## 5. Structure: a five-act play in 300 words

Gustav Freytag (*Die Technik des Dramas*, 1863) mapped the structure of Greek and Shakespearean drama as a pyramid: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, catastrophe/resolution. Two details matter for LinkedIn:
- Freytag's **climax is the turning point in the middle** (Act III), not the ending.
- Modern audiences won't sit through a long falling action. Keep it short.

### The compressed five acts

| Act | Job in the post | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Exposition = Hook** | Open in uncertainty. Hook + twist line inside ~140–210 characters. | 1–2 lines |
| **II. Rising action** | Set the stakes. Present the accepted belief *fairly*, even generously; steelman it. Build pressure. | 3–6 lines |
| **III. Climax = the Turn (volta)** | The reversal: "And yet." / "Here's what the data said instead." The provocative claim lands here, *with* its receipt. | 1–3 lines |
| **IV. Falling action** | Proof and meaning: the gold coins (a number, a scene, a customer's exact words), then what it means for the reader. | 3–6 lines |
| **V. Resolution = Kicker** | One or two lines that click shut, like a sonnet's final couplet. Echo the opening image. Put the strongest word last. Then the CTA. | 1–3 lines |

### Six arcs to choose from

Pick one per post. Match it to the goal.

1. **The Sonnet (contrarian argument).** *Best for authority.* A Shakespearean sonnet is three quatrains and a couplet. The turn comes at line 9 or right before the couplet, and the couplet lands the argument. In Sonnet 130, Shakespeare spends twelve lines refusing every flattering love-poem cliché, then turns with "And yet" to a truer kind of praise. Post version: three short beats describing the convention → "And yet" → a two-line truth.

2. **The Antony (critique without insult).** *Best for levels 3–4.* In *Julius Caesar*, Antony promises not to blame the conspirators, then repeats "Brutus is an honourable man" until praise curdles into its opposite. He asks "Was this ambition?" and lets the crowd answer for themselves, so the conclusion feels like *theirs*. He calls himself "a plain blunt man" and the crowd lowers its guard. Post version: concede the convention's virtue, repeat the concession as the evidence piles up, ask the question, and let the reader convict it. Never name a villain; let the facts do it.

3. **The Prologue (spoiler first).** *Best for reach.* Give away the ending in the hook. That creates dramatic irony: the reader knows *what*, and reads to learn *how*. "We lost our biggest client. Here's the sentence that lost it."

4. **The Tragedy (a mistake and its lesson).** *Best for reach and trust.* The author makes a choice → it goes wrong → the moment of recognition → what they'd do now. Aristotle's reversal and recognition in miniature. Vulnerability here earns the authority to be sharp elsewhere.

5. **The Soliloquy (the inner voice).** *Best for voice and reach.* First person, present tense, thinking out loud. "To be or not to be" is Hamlet *telling* us his indecision, directly. Good for founder doubts and field notes.

6. **The Feature (journalist's arc).** *Best for leads.* Anecdotal lede (a scene) → **nut graf** (the paragraph that says what this is really about and why the reader should care; the *Wall Street Journal* made it standard) → body alternating evidence and counterpoint → kicker that circles back to the opening scene. Ideal for turning an insight or a client case into proof of expertise.

**Goal → default arc:** Authority → Sonnet or Antony. Reach → Prologue, Tragedy or Soliloquy. Leads → Feature (or Sonnet with a proof-heavy Act IV).

**Seeds of curiosity:** end some paragraphs with a very short line that pulls into the next ("That's when it got strange."). Sugarman used this to keep readers sliding. Use it two or three times per post at most.

**Scene-ending couplets:** Shakespeare often closed scenes with a rhymed couplet to build suspense. The post's last two lines should have that ring: balanced, rhythmic, final.

## 6. Sentence craft (newsroom and Shakespeare)

- **Subject and verb first.** Make meaning early; let details branch to the right. (Roy Peter Clark, *Writing Tools*.)
- **Strongest word last.** Shakespeare didn't write "The Queen is dead, my lord." He wrote "The Queen, my lord, is dead." Put the punch at the end of the sentence, the paragraph, and the post.
- **Strong verbs, active voice.** "We found" beats "It was discovered".
- **Vary the pace.** Short sentences for impact and suspense. A longer sentence when you want the reader to travel. Then short again.
- **Climb the ladder of abstraction.** Every abstract claim ("trust drives sales") needs a concrete rung beneath it. Name the actual thing: the cold coffee on the desk, the 47 unread emails, the exact words the client used.
- **Gold coins in the middle.** Don't spend the best detail in the hook. Hide a surprising number, quote or image in Acts III–IV as a reward for reading on.
- **Counting details:** one detail for power, two for contrast, three for wholeness. Four or more becomes a list.
- **Pattern, then twist.** Build a parallel structure (a tricolon, a repeated phrase), then break it on the last beat.
- **Antithesis** (two opposites in one line) is Shakespeare's sharpest tool. Use it once per post for the line people will quote.

**Orwell's six rules** (1946), applied at the edit, never the first draft:
1. Never use a metaphor or figure of speech you're used to seeing in print ("game-changer", "move the needle").
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If you can cut a word, cut it.
4. Active over passive.
5. No jargon when an everyday word exists. Explain industry terms or skip them.
6. Break any rule rather than write something barbarous.

## 7. Sound human (the anti-AI filter)

In the same 1.2M-post dataset, posts that read as AI-written earned about **57% fewer median likes** than human-sounding ones. Much of that gap is *who* posts them, and the likeliest mechanism is readers recognizing the cadence and scrolling past. The post must sound like one specific person.

**Cut on sight:**
- Openers: "In today's fast-paced world", "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing:", "Let that sink in", "I'm humbled to announce".
- Words: delve, landscape, navigate, elevate, unlock, leverage, tapestry, testament, journey, game-changer, transformative, seamless, robust.
- Tics: "It's not X, it's Y" more than once per post; triplets in every sentence; a stack of em dashes (use periods and commas); an emoji at the start of every line; bold "Lesson 1 / Lesson 2" headers; one-line paragraphs for *every* line.
- Endings: "Thoughts?", "Agree?", "What do you think?". These are engagement bait and readers discount them.

**Add:** a real place, a real object, a real number, a line of dialogue someone actually said, a detail only the author could know.

**Default voice** (override with section 0): first person; precise; confident without guru tone; a little wry. The author speaks as a practitioner with evidence, not as someone selling a course. Don't overstate their scale or results.

## 8. LinkedIn mechanics

From the ~1.2M-post analysis (correlational; use as defaults, not laws):
- **Length:** engagement rose steadily with length (47 median likes at 400+ words vs 19 under 25 words). Default **200–350 words**; go to ~450 for a story that earns it. LinkedIn's limit is 3,000 characters.
- **Visual:** image and carousel posts earned about double text-only posts (34 vs 16 median likes). **Always propose one image or carousel concept** in a single line.
- **Links:** an attached link preview card roughly halved impressions (414 vs 795). A URL typed into the body carried no penalty. Put links in the body or the first comment, never as the preview card.
- **Hashtags:** 0 or 1. One tag was fine (35 median likes); six dropped to 15.
- **Emojis:** 0–2, and only if they carry meaning.
- **Polls:** skip. They get seen but not engaged with.
- **Formatting:** LinkedIn doesn't render markdown. Deliver plain text with line breaks. Short paragraphs of 1–3 sentences, not a wall of single lines. Match the author's spelling.

**CTAs by goal:**
- **Leads:** a soft, specific invitation tied to a real, current offer from section 0. "If your next launch has a brief but no story yet, message me."
- **Reach:** a specific question readers can answer from their own life, placed at the *end* (the question penalty applies to first lines).
- **Authority:** no ask. End on the kicker and let the idea sit.

## 9. Truth and brand guardrails

These protect the author's credibility, which is the asset every provocative post spends.

- **Never invent** statistics, quotes, clients, results, scenes or research. Write a clearly marked placeholder like **[real number]** or **[scene you witnessed]** and ask the author to fill it. The shape examples in this skill are illustrations, not facts.
- **Proof scales with heat:** a level 4–5 claim needs a receipt (the author's own data, a scene they witnessed, or a source they can cite).
- **Science and statistics:** use a source you have checked, or the author's research bank (section 12). Say what a study actually tested; don't stretch it.
- **Respect section 0:** use the author's preferred terms, and never put anything marked private into a post.
- **No names without permission.** Don't name prospects, clients, partners, or people from private conversations. "A global retailer" is fine; a company or person's name needs the author's OK.
- **Check current facts.** Offers, numbers and milestones change. When a post depends on them, confirm with the author.

## 10. Deliverable format

Keep the wrapper lean; people often read on their phones. No preamble.

**Write mode** (an idea, story or voice note):
1. **Angle:** one line with the enemy, the claim, the dial level and the goal. Add assumptions if any.
2. **Hooks:** 5 options, each labelled by family. Star the recommended one.
3. **The post:** plain text, ready to paste, using the starred hook.
4. **Dial it:** the hook and turn rewritten one level hotter and one level cooler.
5. **Visual + first comment:** one line for the image or carousel concept; the first-comment text if there's a link.
6. **Fill-ins:** list any [placeholders] the author must replace before posting.

**Rewrite mode** (they paste a draft): keep their voice and their facts. Rebuild the hook and the structure, then give a 3-bullet "what changed and why". Don't lengthen it for the sake of it.

**Hooks-only mode:** 10 hooks across at least 6 families, starred top 3, plus one line on why the winner wins.

**Critique mode:** score out of 5 on Hook, Tension, Specificity, Structure (is there a turn?), Voice (human?), Payoff, and CTA. Then the top 3 fixes and a rewritten opening. Be honest: a flattering critique is useless.

**Comment mode** (a comment on someone else's post): 2–5 sentences. Add one specific insight or finding the post didn't mention, or ask the author a real question. On launch or celebration posts, lead with genuine praise and don't give unsolicited advice. Never "Great post!"

**Sources block:** when a post cites studies, end it with "Sources:" and list each one as: Authors (year). "Title." Journal, volume(issue), pages. Plain text, no links in the post itself.

**Company mentions:** don't add a plug by default. Insight posts often stand better without one. If a line about the author's company would help, offer it separately.

## 11. Final check before delivering

Run through this silently and fix anything that fails:

- [ ] Hook + twist land within ~140–210 characters, as a statement unless a question truly earns it.
- [ ] The 25-word story is clear, and there is a named enemy.
- [ ] There is a real turn (volta) near the middle, not just a list of points.
- [ ] At least one concrete detail and one real, verified specific (or a marked placeholder).
- [ ] The heat matches the dial, and hot claims carry proof.
- [ ] Ideas are provoked, not people; no customer is mocked; nothing private is exposed; no one is named without permission.
- [ ] The strongest word sits at the end of the last line.
- [ ] Nothing from the anti-AI cut list survived.
- [ ] 200–350 words (or justified), 0–1 hashtags, no link preview card, visual proposed.
- [ ] Every study cited is checked, and the Sources block is included.
- [ ] Read aloud: does it sound like the author talking to one smart person across a table?

## 12. Optional: a research bank

Posts with receipts beat posts with opinions. Keep a section here of verified studies the author wants to reuse. For each one, record:

- Authors (year). "Title." Journal, volume(issue), pages.
- One or two sentences on what it actually tested and found, with the key numbers.
- Any limits (sample size, what it didn't test).

Add two more lists:
- **Don't use:** retracted or unverifiable studies.
- **Already used:** which studies and hooks have appeared in posts, so they aren't repeated as the lead.
